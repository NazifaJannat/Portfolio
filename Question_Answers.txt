1. Briefly describe how you typically structure your time or major weekly commitments.

Look, if you saw my calendar, you'd probably think I was crazy. My week is just one big, non-stop juggling act. Between the Students' Union where I'm VP and my role at the NPA, I'm basically always "on." One minute I’m debating political strategy in a stuffy room, the next I’m out on the ground trying to get students to actually talk to each other. That’s a big one for me—trying to bridge that gap between public and private universities. It’s hard work, honestly. People get so stuck in their own bubbles. When things get heated inside the org, I’m usually the one who has to step in, put together a small committee, and basically force everyone to just sit down and listen. It's exhausting, but if we don't do it, everything just stays a total mess.

But I can’t spend all my time on "big" politics. I’d lose my mind. I stay grounded by teaching. I used to spend a ton of my week at this little school in the Korail slum, and man, that place... it just keeps you real. You see the poverty there and it hits you like a ton of bricks. It reminds me why I’m fighting for these policies in the first place. I also do cultural stuff with Udichi Shilpigoshthi—I really believe art can move people in ways that a boring law never could. Toss in some work with garment workers and even small neighborhood things like waste management, and yeah, my schedule is a disaster. But it’s a meaningful disaster, you know? It keeps me connected to what actually matters.

2. How would you describe your level of comfort & experience with technology?

I’m actually pretty tech-savvy, but not in a "I love gadgets" kind of way. For me, tech is a weapon. A tool for the fight. I even built my own portfolio site on GitHub from scratch because I wanted a corner of the internet where my work wouldn't get buried under all the social media noise. 

The July 2024 uprising was where it all clicked. I used digital networking to pull together all these private university students who were basically just floating around without a plan. We started with maybe 40 of us. Within a few days? We were 2,000 strong. We were coordinating across 30 universities in real-time while the world was literally going sideways outside. It was wild, terrifying, but it worked. 

But look, it hasn't all been wins. When I got into the anti-rape movement, the cyberbullying was... well, it was brutal. Total non-stop hate. At first, I’ll be honest, it really messed with my head. I wanted to just delete everything and hide. But looking back, it forced me to grow up and get a thick skin. Real fast. Now, I use social media way more strategically. I’m not just "posting"—I’m sending out calculated messages that actually do something. That mix—using tech for massive scale-ups while dealing with all the online toxicity—totally changed how I handle leadership now.

Once I’m done with the fellowship, I want to take this idea of "biocultural leadership" and actually make it a reality. I’m so tired of seeing progress treated like it has to be the enemy of the environment. I want to build policies that treat human growth and nature as one big, connected system. In Bangladesh, I want to go way beyond just "changing the government"—I want to fix the state itself. I want to help regular people actually understand their rights and how to hold the system accountable.

I also want to use that global network to help bridge the massive divisions I see in the Muslim world. I mean, we’re so fragmented right now, it’s heartbreaking. I’d love to help push for an actual intellectual comeback. And helping Muslim minorities who are being sidelined? That’s something I’m deeply committed to. On a day-to-day level, I want to build teams that have a real internal purpose, so they stay strong even when things get terrifying. Whether I’m fighting for garment workers' dignity or pushing for more power for women in parliament, I’m going to use everything I learn to build a Bangladesh that respects its past but isn't afraid to aim for a much fairer future.

The application captures my strategy and goals well enough, I guess, but I really wish it had asked about the emotional toll this kind of work takes. I lost over 40 fellow students, and I was getting death threats daily. Trying to lead while you're literally terrified for your life—that’s a side of my resilience that a policy question just doesn't touch. 

And finally, I’d have loved to share how my background in literature and art actually runs my politics. My B.A. in English and my work with Udichi Shilpigoshthi? Those aren't hobbies. They’re how I think. I use culture and storytelling to humanize these cold, rigid political systems. To me, that’s what actually makes me a leader—the ability to see the human side of the fight.

6. have you ever participated in any leadership programs, fellowship or trainings. What were they & what did you gain from them? 

Look, being way in the thick of things with the Bangladesh Students' Union has given me more hands-on training than any classroom ever could. It’s not just about some fancy title for me. The org actually runs these sessions to make sure we’re ready for the weight of leading other people. 

A big part of it is just getting real about history and politics. We do these study circles where we really dig into how student movements have shaped this country. Like, from the Language Movement up to the Liberation War. It's heavy stuff, but it anchors you. It makes you realize what justice and democracy actually mean when you’re out on the street. 

I’ve also spent a ton of time on public speaking and debate. I’ll be honest—I used to be terrified. But practicing how to hold an argument in front of a giant, angry crowd? It's a literal lifesaver when you're at a rally or a high-stakes meeting. Then there’s the nuts-and-bolts stuff: managing people, coordinating volunteers, and just making sure a campaign doesn't fall apart at the last second. It's probably the most useful skill I have now. Lately, we’ve even been hitting the digital side—learning how to use social media more strategically. Looking back, these programs didn't just give me "skills." They gave me the actual confidence to step up and lead when things get messy.

9. tell us story what experience shaped who you are today? 

I’ve always been someone who feels things... maybe a bit too much. Even as a little kid, I remember giving away my mom’s brand new clothes to a woman who came to our door asking for help. I just couldn't stand seeing someone go without. I grew up mostly in my own head, buried in Bengali stories and mystery novels. My parents were teachers, so they always pushed me to paint, write, and play sports, which I think helped me finally open up. 

But as I got older, I started noticing how different the world was for women, and it just didn't sit right with me. It felt unfair, you know? I joined the Scouts and university debate just to see different points of view. But Dhaka... that was the real shock. Seeing people living on the street in total, crushing poverty really hit me. I’d just sit and talk to them, and one day I found out they couldn't even afford a simple white cloth for a burial. They couldn't even get their loved ones back to their villages. It was heartbreaking. Truly. I knew right then I couldn't just sit there. That’s what pushed me into the Students Union and later the NPA. I’m still on that path today, and honestly, I’m not stopping.

10. describe a contribution that meaningfully improve a group or community. 

Garment workers are basically the backbone of this whole country, but they get treated like they don't matter. They work insane hours and half the time don't even get their pay on time. In 2023, when I was treasurer for the Students Union, I was seeing this struggle every single day. It wasn't just a news headline for me—it was real lives. 

So I jumped into the minimum wage movement. I was on the ground with them, mostly just listening but also helping them write placards and talking to reporters so their side actually got out there for once. I also spent a lot of time trying to get other folks in civil society to step up and show some support. We were pushing for 23,000 Taka. We didn't get all of it, but we pressured the government enough to move the wage from 8,000 to 12,500. It wasn't perfect, not by a long shot, but it gave a lot of people a little room to breathe. And honestly? They deserve so much more dignity than that.

11. reflect on a challenge that changed you, how did you grow from it? 

Dealing with the backlash was, without a doubt, the hardest part of my journey so far. About two years into my activism, I joined an anti-rape movement, and suddenly I was all over the news. I really didn’t expect it to get so personal. My own family—and people I’d known my whole life—started telling me I shouldn't be an activist. It got so bad that a relative actually banned me from her house. 

And then there was the cyberbullying. It was non-stop. Every time I’d post a photo or an interview, I’d get hit with a wave of hate. At first, it totally messed with my head. I was having these massive ups and downs, feeling completely crushed by the noise. But then I realized—I can’t quit. This is what I’m meant to do. I started reading about my role models and realized they faced the exact same mess. That gave me a huge boost. I leaned on my friends to stay steady and just worked harder. Now, most of those relatives have finally accepted me, and the social media hate? It doesn't really bother me anymore. I learned that in a crisis, you have to just observe and respond instead of letting your emotions run the show.

12. how do you typically handle conflict in a group?

I think sorting out group conflicts is really just about knowing how to actually talk to people. Every situation is different, but as an organizer, I usually follow a simple process. If something blows up, I put together a tiny group of five people—including me—to keep things neutral. 

First, I meet both sides separately. And I just listen. No judgment, no taking sides yet. I try to calm things down and get everything written on paper so we’re actually dealing with facts. Then the committee looks at everything to find a real solution. I act as the mediator when we finally bring everyone together. If it was just a misunderstanding, we clear it up. If someone was actually in the wrong, I help them apologize and make it right. But if someone breaks a major rule or the law, I have to be the one to be clear about the consequences. Keeping it collaborative is the only way I've been able to keep things from falling apart during the tough times.

13. describe a time you took initiative without being asked what did you do? 

In July 2024, during the Quota movement—which turned into a full-on uprising—I was out there prototyping... wait, no, protesting... with the public university students in Dhaka. They were really organized in places like Shahbagh. But I noticed the private university students were just... totally disconnected. I remembered how we failed back in 2018, and it just hit me that I had to do something. 

So I started networking like crazy. From July 6th to the 9th, I was just reaching out to everyone I could. By the 10th, I’d organized the first unified group in Rampura with just 40 students. It totally blew up. By the 16th, we had over 2,000 students at Rampura Bridge. Then July 18th hit, and the police started shooting. We lost over 40 private university students that day. Being there and seeing that bloodshed was just... horrifying. I was getting death threats, but I stayed out there, coordinating with over 30 universities until the government finally fell on August 5th. I ended up being a main coordinator for the whole thing. It was a brutal lesson, but it showed me I can lead even when things are terrifying.

14. describe a time you work with people different from you. 

Back in 2017, when I was starting university, a friend told me about the Jaago Foundation. I really wanted to help, so I signed up to volunteer. For about six months, I was a teacher at their school in the Korail slum in Gulshan. It was a completely different world from anything I’d ever known. 

Those kids didn't have much at all, but that school was their only real shot at a better life. I’d go there twice a week and just get to know them and their world. I taught the younger kids, and we’d make crafts or go play in this little field nearby. Honestly? Those kids were so happy and full of life that it actually made me feel peaceful just being around them. What really stayed with me was seeing them get a real chance to move up and out of that situation. Knowing I helped, even a little bit, felt amazing. And just recently, I heard that one of my old students actually got a full scholarship to go abroad. I can’t even tell you how proud that makes me. It makes all that time in the school feel 100% worth it.

So, I was in Oslo just this past February—actually was there for a seminar at the uni. But honestly, the thing that stuck with me wasn't the lectures. It was this one guy at a market. We actually ended up talking, and he gave me this book of poetry as a "sorry." It taught me more about patience and just seeing people for who they are than any policy paper ever will. Realizing a positive vibe can fix a total disaster? Yeah, that changed me. I realized that being patient can really change how people treat you and that having a positive attitude is where real happiness comes from, no matter what you're facing.

16. what leadership skill do you want to improve the most, why? 

The one thing I really want to nail down is how to actually build a team that doesn't just fall apart. I've seen it way too many times—if the team chemistry isn't there, the project is doomed. First, you’ve got to get people to actually get along, which is way harder than it looks because everyone has their own baggage and history. But without that harmony, you’re just stuck in the mud. 

I also want to get better at helping people find their own "why." So many folks just wait to be told why they should care, and as soon as that external push is gone, they quit. If I can help them find that fire inside themselves, the team stays steady even when things get rocky. And lastly, I need to learn how to keep a team flexible. When things go sideways—and they will—it’s so easy to just freeze up. Being able to pivot without losing your momentum is huge. To me, this isn't just about one project; it’s about building something that actually outlasts me and helps the whole community grow. A team that’s happy and creative is always going to do more than one that’s just checking boxes for a paycheck.

17. what is the most urgent problem in your community and why does it matter to you. 

If I'm being 100% honest, the biggest problem here is that people just don't know how to be "citizens." We have basically zero civic literacy. When you don't know your rights or how the system actually works, everything starts breaking down. I see it every day—look at the trash situation. People just dump it anywhere because they don't feel like the community belongs to them. Then it rains, mosquitoes breed, and suddenly we're all dealing with dengue. 

It’s also about general kindness. I remember coming home late from the hospital once, and the traffic was a nightmare. The gate was locked, so I had to wait. Instead of asking if I was okay, a neighbor just started screaming at me for being out late. That lack of courtesy felt like a slap in the face. Plus, people miss out on so much help because they don't know it exists—like the government Horticulture Centers that sell seeds for pennies. Most folks don't even know they have the right to ask for transparency. Fixing this "citizen gap" is the only way we’re ever going to have a community that actually functions like it should.

18. what challenges facing the wider Muslim world motivate you to act and what role do you want to play? 

The Muslim world is in a really complicated spot right now, and there are three big things that keep me up at night. First, we’re just drifting apart. Every day it feels like we’re more fragmented, stuck in our own little bubbles instead of being one unified community. We need actual ways to talk to each other across those lines, you know? 

Second, we’ve lost our intellectual edge. I mean, we used to lead the world in math and science, but now? We’re trailing behind, and it makes it impossible to have any real impact on the global stage. And finally, the way Muslim minorities are treated in so many countries is just heartbreaking. They face so much discrimination, and I really want to be part of the generation that fixes that. I want to play a role in intellectual revitalization—bringing back that culture of learning and unified dialogue. We need to move past the fragmentation and start thinking collectively again.

19. what leadership quality do you believe the world needs more of right now and why does it matter to you? 

Okay, look, I know 'biocultural leadership' sounds like some fancy PhD term, but it’s actually just common sense that we’ve all forgotten. We act like making money and saving the trees are these two separate fights. They’re not. It’s the same ecosystem. We’re in this mess because we keep pillaging nature like it’s a bottomless bank account. 

I want to be the kind of leader who threads the needle—someone who says 'yeah, we need progress, but we can't burn the house down to keep warm.' It matters to me because I want there to actually be a world left for the next generation. It’s about dealing with the root rot, not just painting over the cracks. If people see you’re fighting for their prosperity and their planet, they’ll follow you anywhere. That kind of leadership? That’s what’s going to actually save us.
