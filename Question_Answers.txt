1. Briefly describe how you typically structure your time or major weekly commitments.

Honestly, my schedule is pretty much a whirlwind. Because of my roles with the Students Union and the NPA, I’m constantly jumping between political strategy, organizing on the ground, and fighting for student rights. It’s a lot to manage, but I try to keep it disciplined. A big chunk of my week is dedicated to advocating for campus democracy and trying to get folks from both public and private universities to actually talk to each other. When things get messy within the organization—which they do—I’m usually the one putting together small committees to mediate. I'm a big believer in just sitting down, listening to both sides, and figuring out a way forward that doesn't leave everyone bitter. 

But I also make sure I don't lose touch with what's actually happening on the street. I’m still really involved in teaching kids who don't have much. I used to spend a lot of my time at a school in the Korail slum, and honestly, that’s what keeps me grounded. It reminds me why I’m doing all this policy work in the first place—to deal with the real-world poverty people are facing right now. I also spend time organizing cultural events with Udichi Shilpigoshthi because I think art and storytelling are just as powerful as any law for changing how people think. My debating background definitely helps when I have to stand up in front of a crowd and explain what our community needs. I also spend a lot of time with garment workers, helping them write their campaign stuff and making sure the media actually pays attention to them. I even try to help out with smaller things like waste management in the neighborhood. It’s a lot to balance, for sure, but it keeps my leadership connected to what’s real.

2. How would you describe your level of comfort & experience with technology?

I’m actually very comfortable with tech. I don't just use it; I see it as a total necessity for the kind of political and social work I do. I even built and keep up my own portfolio site on GitHub—I wanted a single place where people could see everything I’m working on without it getting lost in the social media noise. 

Tech was basically a lifesaver during the July 2024 uprising. I used digital networking to pull together private university students who really didn't have any organization yet. We started with just 40 people and somehow turned that into a group of over 2,000 in only a few days. We were coordinating across 30 different universities in real-time, all while things were moving incredibly fast on the ground. It was intense, but we couldn't have done it without those digital tools.

That said, I’ve definitely seen the ugly side of being online. When I got involved with the anti-rape movement and started getting some media attention, the cyberbullying was just non-stop and honestly pretty brutal. It really messed with me at first—it was a lot to handle emotionally. But looking back, it actually forced me to grow up fast and develop a much thicker skin. I learned how to tune out all that online toxicity and stay focused on my mission. Now, I use social media way more strategically. It’s not about reacting anymore; it’s about sending out calculated, impactful messages that actually move the needle. Dealing with both the power of tech for organizing and the mess of online hate has really shaped how I lead in the digital space today.

Look, to be totally real with you, this USD 10,000 grant is a huge deal for me. It’s what’s going to make this work sustainable. I grew up in a fairly modest home—both my parents were teachers—so my activism has always been driven by empathy rather than having money to throw around. It would allow me to really dive into the skills I want to sharpen—like building better policies, getting better at public communication, and forming a global network. It would also give me the freedom to explore this idea of "biocultural leadership" I’m so passionate about—finding that balance between progress and nature—without the constant stress of my immediate livelihood. For me, this isn’t just some prize; it’s an investment in my capacity to actually drive change and bridge the gaps I see in the Muslim world and back home in Bangladesh.

4. What do you hope to accomplish after completing the AGL fellowship?

After the fellowship, my main goal is to really push for systemic reform through what I call "biocultural leadership." I want to help create policies that recognize that human progress and environmental health are parts of the same ecosystem. I’m tired of seeing growth that just destroys the planet. I want to take the policy and communication skills I gain and apply them to the real root issues we have in Bangladesh—to go beyond just changing the people in charge and actually fix how the state works and help regular people understand their rights.

I also want to use that global network to help fix the stagnation I see in the wider Muslim world. I’d love to help bridge some of the divisions within our communities and push for a real intellectual comeback. Helping Muslim minorities who are being sidelined or discriminated against is also something that's really close to my heart. On a more practical level, I want to build teams that have a real sense of purpose inside them, so they don't just quit when things get hard. Whether it’s fighting for the dignity of garment workers or pushing for more funding and power for women in parliament, I plan to use everything I learn to build a Bangladesh that stays true to its roots while aiming for a much fairer and more sustainable future.

6.have you ever participated in any leadership programs, fellowship or trainings. What were they & what did you gain from them? 

Being a leader in the Bangladesh Students' Union has definitely given me some real hands-on training. It’s not just about titles; the organization runs these sessions to actually prep us for the responsibility that comes with leading others.

A big chunk of it is just straight-up history and politics. We do these study circles and workshops where we dig into how student movements shaped Bangladesh—like the Language Movement or the Liberation War. It’s heavy stuff, but it really anchors you in what justice and democracy actually mean.

I’ve also spent a lot of time on public speaking and debate stuff. It was nerve-wracking at first, but practicing how to deliver a solid speech or handle an argument in front of a Crowd is a lifesaver when you're at a rally or a big meeting.

Then there’s the practical side—managing people and organizing. We learn the nuts and bolts of coordinating volunteers, planning campaigns, and just making sure a team doesn't fall apart. It’s probably the most useful part for day-to-day work.

We also get into policy discussions and research, which really sharpens how you think about social issues. And lately, we've been hitting the digital side of things too—learning how to use social media like Facebook and YouTube to get our message out to more people.

Honestly, looking back, these programs didn't just give me skills; they gave me the confidence to actually step up and lead with a real sense of responsibility.

9. tell us story what experience shaped who you are today? 

I guess I’ve just always been the type of person who feels things really deeply. It's actually a bit much sometimes if I'm being real. Like, I still remember giving away my mom's brand new clothes to a woman who came to our door asking for help. I was just a kid but I couldn't help it. Growing up I was mostly in my own head with books, mostly Bengali stories and mystery novels. My parents were teachers so they always pushed me to paint and write and play sports, and I think that’s where I started to open up. Then later on I noticed how different things were for women and it just didn't sit right. It felt unfair. I joined the scouts and university debate to see other points of view and it really sharpened my brain. But Dhaka was the real shock. Seeing folks living on the street in total poverty really hit me. I’d talk to them and one day I found out they couldn't even afford a white cloth for a burial or even get a body back to their village. It was heartbreaking. Truly. I knew then I had to do something. So I joined the Students Union and then this NPA network. I’m still on that path today and I'm not stopping.

10. describe a contribution that meaningfully improve a group or community. 

Garment workers are basically the reason Bangladesh's economy even works but they get treated so badly. They work these crazy long hours and then don't even get their wages half the time. In 2023 I was treasurer for the Students Union and I was seeing this struggle firsthand every day. It wasn't just a news thing, it was real lives. So I got into the minimum wage movement. I was on the ground with them, mostly just listening and helping them write placards or talking to the press so their side actually got out there. I also tried to get other people from civil society to step up and show some support. We were pushing for 23,000 Taka. We didn't get all of it but we pressured the government to move the wage from 8,000 to 12,500. It wasn't perfect but it gave people room to breathe. And honestly they deserve a lot more dignity than that.

11. reflect on a challenge that changed you, how did you grow from it? 

Dealing with the backlash was honestly the hardest part. About two years into my activism I joined an anti-rape movement and suddenly I was on TV and in the papers. I didn't expect it to get so personal. My family and people I knew started saying I shouldn't be an activist. It got so bad that one of my sister's relatives actually banned me from her house. Plus the cyberbullying was just non-stop. Every photo or interview I posted got hit with hate. At first it really messed with my head. I was having these massive ups and downs and I felt totally crushed by the online abuse. But then I realized I couldn't just quit. This is what I’m meant to do. I started reading about my role models and saw they faced the same mess and that gave me a huge boost. I leaned on my friends to stay steady and I just worked harder. Now most of those relatives have finally accepted me and the social media noise doesn't bother me much. I learned that in a crisis you just have to observe and respond instead of just reacting with your emotions.

12. how do you typically handle conflict in a group?

I think fixing conflicts in a group is really just about knowing how to talk to people. Every situation is different but as an organizer I usually follow a simple process. If something blows up I put together a small group of five people, including me, to try and stay neutral. First I just meet both sides separately and listen. No judgment. I try to calm things down and get everything on paper so we have the facts. Then the committee looks at everything to find a solution. I act as the mediator when we bring everyone together. If it’s just a misunderstanding we clear it up. If someone was wrong I help them apologize. But if it’s a big rule break or against the law I have to be clear about the punishments and our rules. Keeping it collaborative is really the only way I've been able to keep things together during tough times.

13. describe a time you took initiative without being asked what did you do? 

In July 2024 during the Quota movement, which turned into a full on uprising, I was out there protesting with the public university students in Dhaka. We were in Shahbagh and Nilkhet and they were really organized. But I saw the private university students were just totally unorganized. I remembered 2018 when we failed and it just hit me that I had to do something. So I started networking like crazy from July 6th to the 9th. By the 10th I’d organized the first unified group in Rampura with 40 students. It blew up. By the 16th there were over 2,000 students at Rampura Bridge. Then July 18th came and the police started shooting. We lost over 40 private university students that day. Being there and seeing that bloodshed was horrifying. I was getting death threats too but I stayed out there coordinating with over 30 universities until the government fell on August 5th. I ended up as one of the main coordinators for the whole uprising. It was a brutal lesson but it showed me I can lead even when things are terrifying.

14. describe a time you work with people different from you. 

Back in 2017 when I was in university a friend told me about the Jaago Foundation. I really wanted to help out so I joined as a volunteer. For about six months I was a teacher at their school in the Korail slum in Gulshan. It was a completely different world from what I was used to. The kids there didn't have much at all but that school was like their only real shot at a better life. I’d go there twice a week and just get to know them and how they lived. Mostly I taught the younger kids and we’d make crafts together. There was this little field nearby where we’d play games too. Honestly those kids were so happy and cheerful it actually made me feel really peaceful just being around them. What really stuck with me was seeing them get a real chance to move up in the world. Knowing I helped even a little bit feels great. And just recently I heard that one of my old students actually got a full scholarship to go abroad. It made me so incredibly proud to know I was part of that school.

15. tell us something new you learn recently that change your perspective. 

Not long ago I went to Norway for a seminar at the University of Oslo. I was there for about a week in February 2026. One day I was at this open market and found an old guy selling books and stuff from his truck. I picked out some souvenirs and asked for a book in English but he said it was still packed up. While I was waiting I realized his left side was paralyzed and he was really struggling to move these heavy baskets. I just naturally tried to help him out but he suddenly just started screaming 'Go back! Go back!' at me. I was so shocked and honestly really embarrassed because everyone was looking at me. But I stayed quiet and paid for my stuff and just asked for some paper to wrap my souvenirs for the flight home. After I finished his whole mood just changed. He asked if I was some kind of activist because he was surprised I stayed so patient after he yelled. He actually apologized and told me how he’s just proud of living his life on his own even with his physical problems. Before I left he even gave me a book of poems as a gift. It really changed how I see things. I realized that being patient can really change how people treat you and that having a positive attitude is where real happiness comes from no matter what you're facing.

16. what leadership skill do you want to improve the most, why? 

The one thing I really want to get better at as a leader is how to actually build a team that works. I've seen over and over that if a team doesn't have a few big things right it just falls apart. First off is just getting people to actually get along. It’s a lot harder than it sounds because everyone comes from different places and sees things differently. But without that harmony you just can’t get anything done. I also want to learn how to help people find their own reason for doing the work. So many folks just wait for someone else to tell them why they should care and when that stops they just quit or lose interest. If I can help them find that internal drive the team stays a lot steadier. And finally I really need to learn how to keep a team flexible. When things go wrong it's so easy to just freeze up and lose your strength so being able to adjust is huge. To me mastering this isn't just about one project. It’s about building something that actually lasts and helps the whole community or even the country grow. A team that stays happy and creative is way more productive in the long run.

17. what is the most urgent problem in your community and why does it matter to you. 

If I'm being 100% real the biggest problem here is that people just don't know how to be citizens. We have zero training for what I call civic literacy. When you don't know your rights or how to work with the state everything starts falling apart. I see it every day. Like with all the trash. People just dump it anywhere because they don't feel responsible for the community. Then the rains come and mosquitoes breed and suddenly we’re all at risk. Including me. It’s also about how we treat each other. I remember coming home late from the hospital one time and the traffic was a nightmare. The gate was locked so I had to wait for my brother. And a neighbor just started screaming at me for being out late. No 'are you okay?' or 'need help?' Just yelling. That lack of courtesy was a shock. Plus people miss out on help. The government has these Horticulture Centers that sell seeds for almost nothing but farmers here don't know they exist. Most folks don't even know they can ask for transparency. To me fixing this is the only way to make a community that actually works.

18. what challenges facing the wider Muslim world motivate you to act and what role do you want to play? 

The Muslim world is going through a lot right now and it's definitely complicated. Three things really drive me to act. First we're just drifting apart. Every day we feel more fragmented and stuck in our own groups instead of being one community. We need easy ways to talk to each other. Second we lost our intellectual edge. We used to lead in math and science but that’s gone now and it makes it hard to have an actual impact. And finally the way Muslim minorities are sidelined in so many countries is just heartbreaking. They face so much discrimination and I really want to find a way to fix that.

19. what leadership quality do you believe the world needs more of right now and why does it matter to you? 

I'm convinced what the world really needs right now is biocultural leadership. It sounds like a big term but it’s actually pretty simple. It’s about leading in a way that knows human progress and nature aren't separate things. Our tech and culture are part of the same ecosystem that keeps us alive. We're in a mess because we lost that balance. We need factories but they're melting the ice caps. We can't just stop industry so we need leaders who can thread the needle and make policies that keep us prosperous without killing the planet. This matters to me because nature isn't just something to be looted. It's a partner. It’s about fixing the cause instead of just the symptoms. Plus it gives the next generation a chance at a future. When people see a leader fighting for both they’re proud to follow. Honestly it’s the only path I see for a world that's actually worth living in.
