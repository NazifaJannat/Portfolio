1. Briefly describe how you typically structure your time or major weekly commitments.

My week? Honestly, it’s a total circus. I’m the VP for the Students' Union and the spokesperson for the NPA, so I’m basically living out of my backpack and running on caffeine most days. It’s a lot of "go go go"—one minute I’m in a high-stakes strategy meeting, the next I’m on the ground trying to get students from totally different backgrounds to actually sit in a room and talk. Bridging that public vs. private university gap is my main mission, but man, it is like pulling teeth sometimes. When things get messy within the org—which happens way more than I’d like—I’m the one who ends up forming these tiny five-person truce-committees to stop the infighting. It’s exhausting, but someone’s gotta do it.

To keep from actually Losing my mind, I teach. I used to spend a huge chunk of my week at a school in the Korail slum. That place... it’s real. It’s raw. Seeing that level of poverty isn’t just a "lesson," it’s a wake-up call that reminds me why I’m even doing this political stuff. I also do cultural work with Udichi Shilpigoshthi because I genuinely believe a song or a play can change a mind faster than a 50-page policy paper. Throw in my work with garment workers and even random neighborhood things like waste management, and yeah, my schedule is a wreck. But it’s my wreck, and it keeps me human.

2. How would you describe your level of comfort & experience with technology?

I’m tech-savvy, sure, but I don't treat tech like a hobby. I treat it like a weapon for change. I actually built my own portfolio on GitHub because I wanted a place for my work to live that wasn't controlled by some algorithm or buried under social media junk. 

The 2024 uprising was the real test. I was using digital networks to pull together hundreds of private university students who were basically just unorganized and scattered. We started with maybe 40 people. Within a few days? We were more than 2,000 strong. We were coordinating across 30 different campuses in real-time while the world outside was just... exploding. It was the most intense thing I've ever lived through, but it showed me what digital tools can actually do when the stakes are life and death.

But man, the internet can be a dark place too. When I got into the anti-rape movement, the cyberbullying was... it was just brutal. Just non-stop, disgusting hate. At first, I won't lie, it really got under my skin. I wanted to just disappear. But looking back, that mess is what forced me to grow up. It gave me a thick skin that I don't think I would've found any other way. Now, I use social media like a surgeon. I don't just "post"—I send out targeted, calculated messages that I know will actually move the needle. That mix—using tech to scale movements while surviving that online toxicity—is what really shaped how I lead today.

Look, let’s be totally real: this $10,000 grant is a absolute game-changer for me. It’s the difference between me being able to do this work and having to quit. Plus, it would let me really dig into my "biocultural leadership" idea—you know, that balance between prosperity and nature—without the constant, nagging weight of "how am I going to afford rent next month?". For me, this isn’t just a prize. It’s a literal lifeline so I can keep the fight going.

4. What do you hope to accomplish after completing the AGL fellowship?

After the fellowship, I want to take this whole "biocultural leadership" thing and turn it into real-world policy. I am so done with seeing "progress" treated like the enemy of the environment. I want to build systems where human growth and nature actually work together like a single machine. In Bangladesh, I’m looking way beyond just "changing the guard"—I want to fix the state itself. I want to help regular people actually understand what their rights are and how to make the system actually work for them.

I also want to use the global network I build to help bridge the massive divisions I see in the Muslim world. It’s heartbreaking how fragmented we are right now. I want to push for an actual intellectual comeback. And helping Muslim minorities who are being treated like second-class citizens? That is something I am deeply, personally committed to. On a day-to-day level, I want to build teams that have a real, internal sense of "why" so they don't just fold when things get terrifying. Whether I’m fighting for garment workers or pushing for more power for women in parliament, I’m going to use everything I learn to build a Bangladesh that respects its past but isn't afraid of a much fairer future.

5. If not fully, what do you wish the application had asked (or not asked) to better reflect who you are?

The application gets my strategy and my goals, I guess, but I really wish it had asked about the actual emotional cost of this life. Activism isn’t just about the wins; it’s about what you have to leave behind. I wish I could’ve talked about the isolation. When I became a public face for the anti-rape movement, my own family literally turned their backs on me. I was even banned from a relative's house. It is incredibly hard to stay focused on a movement when your own support system is crumbling at home.

I also wish there was space to talk about the actual trauma of being on the front lines. During the 2024 uprising, I wasn't just "coordinating." I was seeing people die right in front of me. I lost over 40 fellow students, and I was getting death threats every single day. Trying to lead people when you are literally terrified for your own life—that’s a side of resilience that no policy question can ever really touch. 

And finally, I’d have loved to show how my background in literature and art is the actual engine of my politics. My B.A. in English and my work with Udichi Shilpigoshthi? Those aren't just "hobbies." They are how I think. I use culture and storytelling to humanize these cold, rigid political systems. To me, that’s what actually makes me a leader—the ability to see the human being inside the fight.

6. have you ever participated in any leadership programs, fellowship or trainings. What were they & what did you gain from them? 

Honestly, being tossed into the deep end with the Bangladesh Students' Union has been a better "training program" than any classroom. It’s not just a fancy title on a CV. The org actually runs these deep-dive sessions to make sure we’re ready for the actual weight of leading other people. 

A huge part of it is just getting real about history and politics. We do these study circles where we really tear apart how student movements have shaped this country. I’m talking from the Language Movement all the way to the Liberation War. It’s heavy stuff, but it anchors you. It makes you realize what justice and democracy actually look like when you’re out on the street with thousands of people. 

I’ve also spent a ton of time on public speaking and debate. I’ll be honest—I used to be terrified of a microphone. But practicing how to hold an argument in front of a giant, angry crowd? It’s a literal lifesaver when you’re at a rally or a high-stakes meeting. Then there’s the "boring" stuff that actually matters: managing people, coordinating volunteers, and making sure a campaign doesn't fall apart at the last second. Looking back, these programs didn't just give me "skills." They gave me the actual confidence to stand up and lead even when things are a total mess.

9. tell us story what experience shaped who you are today? 

I’ve always been someone who feels things... maybe a bit too much, if I'm being real. Even as a tiny kid, I remember giving away my mom’s brand new clothes to a woman who came to our door asking for help. I just couldn't stand seeing someone go without. I grew up mostly in my own head, buried in Bengali stories and mystery novels. My parents were teachers, so they always pushed me to paint, write, and play sports, which I think is the only reason I eventually opened up. 

But as I got older, I started noticing how different the world was for women, and it just didn't sit right with me. It felt unfair, you know? I joined the Scouts and university debate just to see different points of view. But Dhaka... that was the real shock. Seeing people living on the street in total, crushing poverty really hit me. I’d just sit and talk to them, and one day I found out they couldn't even afford a simple white cloth for a burial. They couldn't even get their loved ones back to their villages. It was heartbreaking. Truly. I knew right then I couldn't just sit there. That’s what pushed me into the Students Union and later the NPA. I’m still on that path today, and honestly, I’m not stopping.

10. describe a contribution that meaningfully improve a group or community. 

Garment workers in Bangladesh are basically the backbone of this whole country, but they get treated like they don't matter at all. They work insane hours and half the time don't even get their pay on time. In 2023, when I was treasurer for the Students Union, I was seeing this struggle every single day. It wasn't just a news headline for me—it was real lives, real families. 

So I jumped into the minimum wage movement. I was on the ground with them, mostly just listening but also helping them write placards and talking to reporters so their side actually got out there for once. I also spent a lot of time trying to get other folks in civil society to step up and show some support. We were pushing for 23,000 Taka. We didn't get all of it, but we pressured the government enough to move the wage from 8,000 to 12,500. It wasn't perfect, not even close, but it gave a lot of people a little room to breathe. And honestly? They deserve so much more dignity than that.

11. reflect on a challenge that changed you, how did you grow from it? 

Dealing with the backlash was, without a doubt, the hardest thing I've ever had to handle. About two years into my activism, I joined an anti-rape movement, and suddenly I was all over the news. I really didn’t expect it to get so personal, so fast. My own family—and people I’d known my whole life—started telling me I shouldn't be an activist. It got so bad that a relative actually banned me from her house. 

And then there was the cyberbullying. It was non-stop. Every time I’d post a photo or an interview, I’d get hit with a wave of hate. At first, it totally messed with my head. I was having these massive ups and downs, feeling completely crushed by the noise. But then I realized—I can’t quit. This is what I’m meant to do. I started reading about my role models and realized they faced the exact same mess. That gave me a huge boost. I leaned on my friends to stay steady and just worked harder. Now, most of those relatives have finally accepted me, and the social media hate? It doesn't really bother me anymore. I learned that in a crisis, you have to just observe and respond instead of letting your emotions run the show.

12. how do you typically handle conflict in a group?

Honestly, sorting out group conflicts is really just about knowing how to actually talk to people when they're angry. Every situation is different, but as an organizer, I usually follow a simple process. If something blows up, I put together a tiny group of five people—including me—to keep things neutral and balanced. 

First, I meet both sides separately. And I just listen. No judgment, no taking sides yet. I try to calm things down and get everything written on paper so we’re actually dealing with facts rather than just feelings. Then the committee looks at everything to find a real solution. I act as the mediator when we finally bring everyone together. If it was just a misunderstanding, we clear it up. If someone was actually in the wrong, I help them apologize and make it right. But if someone breaks a major rule or the law, I have to be the one to be clear about the consequences. Keeping it collaborative is the only way I've been able to keep things from falling apart during the tough times.

13. describe a time you took initiative without being asked what did you do? 

In July 2024, during the Quota movement—which turned into a full-on uprising—I was out there prototyping... wait, no, I mean protesting... with the public university students in Dhaka. They were really organized in places like Shahbagh. But I noticed the private university students were just... totally disconnected and scattered. I remembered how we failed back in 2018, and it just hit me that I had to do something right then. 

So I started networking like crazy. From July 6th to the 9th, I was basically living on my phone, reaching out to everyone I could. By the 10th, I’d organized the first unified group in Rampura with just 40 students. It totally blew up. By the 16th, we had over 2,000 students at Rampura Bridge. Then July 18th hit, and the police started shooting. We lost over 40 private university students that day. Being there and seeing that bloodshed was just... horrifying. I was getting death threats, but I stayed out there, coordinating with over 30 universities until the government finally fell on August 5th. I ended up being a main coordinator for the whole thing. It was a brutal lesson, but it showed me I can lead even when things are terrifying.

14. describe a time you work with people different from you. 

Back in 2017, when I was just starting university, a friend told me about the Jaago Foundation. I really wanted to help, so I signed up to volunteer. For about six months, I was a teacher at their school in the Korail slum in Gulshan. It was a completely different world from anything I’d ever known. 

Those kids didn't have much at all—no big houses, no high-tech toys—but that school was their only real shot at a better life. I’d go there twice a week and just get to know them and their world. I taught the younger kids, and we’d make crafts or go play in this little field nearby. Honestly? Those kids were so happy and full of life that it actually made me feel peaceful just being around them. What really stayed with me was seeing them get a real chance to move up and out of that situation. Knowing I helped, even a little bit, felt amazing. And just recently, I heard that one of my old students actually got a full scholarship to go abroad. I can’t even tell you how proud that makes me. It makes all that time in the school feel 100% worth it.

15. tell us something new you learn recently that change your perspective. 

So, I was in Oslo just this past February—actually was there for a seminar at the uni. But honestly, the thing that stuck with me wasn't the lectures. It was this one guy at a market. Realizing a positive vibe can fix a total disaster? Yeah, that changed me. I realized that being patient can really change how people treat you and that having a positive attitude is where real happiness comes from, no matter what you're facing.

16. what leadership skill do you want to improve the most, why? 

The one thing I really, really want to nail down is how to actually build a team that doesn't just crumble when things get tough. I've seen it way too many times—if the team chemistry isn't there, the project is doomed before it even starts. First, you’ve got to get people to actually get along, which is way harder than it looks because everyone has their own baggage and history. But without that harmony, you’re just stuck in the mud. 

I also want to get better at helping people find their own "why." So many folks just wait to be told why they should care, and as soon as that external push is gone, they quit. If I can help them find that fire inside themselves, the team stays steady even when things get rocky. And lastly, I need to learn how to keep a team flexible. When things go sideways—and they will—it’s so easy to just freeze up. Being able to pivot without losing your momentum is huge. To me, this isn't just about one project; it’s about building something that actually outlasts me and helps the whole community grow. A team that’s happy and creative is always going to do more than one that’s just checking boxes for a paycheck.

17. what is the most urgent problem in your community and why does it matter to you. 

If I'm being 100% honest, the biggest problem here is that people just don't know how to be "citizens." We have basically zero civic literacy. When you don't know your rights or how the system actually works, everything starts breaking down. I see it every day—look at the trash situation. People just dump it anywhere because they don't feel like the community belongs to them. Then it rains, mosquitoes breed, and suddenly we're all dealing with dengue. 

It’s also about general kindness. I remember coming home late from the hospital once, and the traffic was a nightmare. The gate was locked, so I had to wait. Instead of asking if I was okay, a neighbor just started screaming at me for being out late. That lack of courtesy felt like a slap in the face. Plus, people miss out on so much help because they don't know it exists—like the government Horticulture Centers that sell seeds for pennies. Most folks don't even know they have the right to ask for transparency. Fixing this "citizen gap" is the only way we’re ever going to have a community that actually functions like it should.

18. what challenges facing the wider Muslim world motivate you to act and what role do you want to play? 

The Muslim world is in a really complicated spot right now, and there are three big things that keep me up at night. First, we’re just drifting apart. Every day it feels like we’re more fragmented, stuck in our own little bubbles instead of being one unified community. We need actual ways to talk to each other across those lines, you know? 

Second, we’ve lost our intellectual edge. I mean, we used to lead the world in math and science, but now? We’re trailing behind, and it makes it impossible to have any real impact on the global stage. And finally, the way Muslim minorities are treated in so many countries is just heartbreaking. They face so much discrimination, and I really want to be part of the generation that fixes that. I want to play a role in intellectual revitalization—bringing back that culture of learning and unified dialogue. We need to move past the fragmentation and start thinking collectively again.

19. what leadership quality do you believe the world needs more of right now and why does it matter to you? 

Okay, look, I know 'biocultural leadership' sounds like some fancy PhD term, but it’s actually just common sense that we’ve all forgotten. We act like making money and saving the trees are these two separate fights. They’re not. It’s the same ecosystem. We’re in this mess because we keep pillaging nature like it’s a bottomless bank account. 

I want to be the kind of leader who threads the needle—someone who says 'yeah, we need progress, but we can't burn the house down to keep warm.' It matters to me because I want there to actually be a world left for the next generation. It’s about dealing with the root rot, not just painting over the cracks. If people see you’re fighting for their prosperity and their planet, they’ll follow you anywhere. That kind of leadership? That’s what’s going to actually save us.
