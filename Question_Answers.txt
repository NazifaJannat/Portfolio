1. Briefly describe how you typically structure your time or major weekly commitments.

My week? Honestly, it’s a total circus. I’m the VP for the Students' Union and the spokesperson for the NPA, so I’m basically living out of my backpack and running on caffeine most days. It’s a lot of "go go go"—one minute I’m in a high-stakes strategy meeting, the next I’m on the ground trying to get students from totally different backgrounds to actually sit in a room and talk. Bridging that public vs. private university gap is my main mission, but man, it is like pulling teeth sometimes. When things get messy within the org—which happens way more than I’d like—I’m the one who ends up forming these tiny five-person truce-committees to stop the infighting. It’s exhausting, but someone’s gotta do it.

To keep from actually Losing my mind, I teach. I used to spend a huge chunk of my week at a school in the Korail slum. That place... it’s real. It’s raw. Seeing that level of poverty isn’t just a "lesson," it’s a wake-up call that reminds me why I’m even doing this political stuff. I also do cultural work with Udichi Shilpigoshthi because I genuinely believe a song or a play can change a mind faster than a 50-page policy paper. Throw in my work with garment workers and even random neighborhood things like waste management, and yeah, my schedule is a wreck. But it’s my wreck, and it keeps me human.

2. How would you describe your level of comfort & experience with technology?

I’m tech-savvy, sure, but I don't treat tech like a hobby. I treat it like a weapon for change. I actually built my own portfolio on GitHub because I wanted a place for my work to live that wasn't controlled by some algorithm or buried under social media junk. 

The 2024 uprising was the real test. I was using digital networks to pull together hundreds of private university students who were basically just unorganized and scattered. We started with maybe 40 people. Within a few days? We were more than 2,000 strong. We were coordinating across 30 different campuses in real-time while the world outside was just... exploding. It was the most intense thing I've ever lived through, but it showed me what digital tools can actually do when the stakes are life and death.

But man, the internet can be a dark place too. When I got into the anti-rape movement, the cyberbullying was... it was brutal. Just non-stop, disgusting hate. At first, I won't lie, it really got under my skin. I wanted to just disappear. But looking back, that mess is what forced me to grow up. It gave me a thick skin that I don't think I would've found any other way. Now, I use social media like a surgeon. I don't just "post"—I send out targeted, calculated messages that I know will actually move the needle. That mix—using tech to scale movements while surviving that online toxicity—is what really shaped how I lead today.

Look, let’s be totally real: this $10,000 grant is a absolute game-changer for me. It’s the difference between me being able to do this work and having to quit. I come from a family of teachers—modest, hardworking, but zero "safety net." My activism with the BSU and the NPA? That’s all volunteer. I don't get paid a cent to fight for these reforms. I’ve managed big budgets before as a Finance Secretary, so I’m good with money, but my own personal bank account is pretty much a joke.

If I get this grant, I can finally stop stressing about how I'm going to pay for basic stuff and actually focus 100% on the Azm fellowship. It would give me the breathing room to master policy work, get better at public speaking, and build those global connections I’m hungry for. Plus, it would let me really dig into my "biocultural leadership" idea—you know, that balance between prosperity and nature—without the constant, nagging weight of "how am I going to afford rent next month?". For me, this isn’t just a prize. It’s a literal lifeline so I can keep the fight going.

4. What do you hope to accomplish after completing the AGL fellowship?

After the fellowship, I want to take this whole "biocultural leadership" thing and turn it into real-world policy. I am so done with seeing "progress" treated like the enemy of the environment. I want to build systems where human growth and nature actually work together like a single machine. In Bangladesh, I’m looking way beyond just "changing the guard"—I want to fix the state itself. I want to help regular people actually understand what their rights are and how to make the system actually work for them.

I also want to use the global network I build to help bridge the massive divisions I see in the Muslim world. It’s heartbreaking how fragmented we are right now. I want to push for an actual intellectual comeback. And helping Muslim minorities who are being treated like second-class citizens? That is something I am deeply, personally committed to. On a day-to-day level, I want to build teams that have a real, internal sense of "why" so they don't just fold when things get terrifying. Whether I’m fighting for garment workers or pushing for more power for women in parliament, I’m going to use everything I learn to build a Bangladesh that respects its past but isn't afraid of a much fairer future.

5. If not fully, what do you wish the application had asked (or not asked) to better reflect who you are?

The application gets my strategy and my goals, I guess, but I really wish it had asked about the actual emotional cost of this life. Activism isn’t just about the wins; it’s about what you have to leave behind. I wish I could’ve talked about the isolation. When I became a public face for the anti-rape movement, my own family literally turned their backs on me. I was even banned from a relative's house. It is incredibly hard to stay focused on a movement when your own support system is crumbling at home.

I also wish there was space to talk about the actual trauma of being on the front lines. During the 2024 uprising, I wasn't just "coordinating." I was seeing people die right in front of me. I lost over 40 fellow students, and I was getting death threats every single day. Trying to lead people when you are literally terrified for your own life—that’s a side of resilience that no policy question can ever really touch. 

And finally, I’d have loved to show how my background in literature and art is the actual engine of my politics. My B.A. in English and my work with Udichi Shilpigoshthi? Those aren't just "hobbies." They are how I think. I use culture and storytelling to humanize these cold, rigid political systems. To me, that’s what actually makes me a leader—the ability to see the human being inside the fight.

6. have you ever participated in any leadership programs, fellowship or trainings. What were they & what did you gain from them? 

Honestly, being tossed into the deep end with the Bangladesh Students' Union has been a better "training program" than any classroom. It’s not just a fancy title on a CV. The org actually runs these sessions to make sure we’re ready for the actual weight of leading other people. 

A huge part of it is just getting real about history and politics. We do these study circles where we really tear apart how student movements have shaped this country. I’m talking from the Language Movement all the way to the Liberation War. It’s heavy stuff, but it anchors you. It makes you realize what justice and democracy actually look like when you’re out on the street with thousands of people. 

I’ve also spent a ton of time on public speaking and debate. I’ll be honest—I used to be terrified of a microphone. But practicing how to hold an argument in front of a giant, angry crowd? It’s a literal lifesaver when you’re at a rally or a high-stakes meeting. Then there’s the "boring" stuff that actually matters: managing people, coordinating volunteers, and making sure a campaign doesn't fall apart at the last second. Looking back, these programs didn't just give me "skills." They gave me the actual confidence to stand up and lead even when things are a total mess.

9. tell us story what experience shaped who you are today? 

I’ve always been someone who feels things... maybe a bit too much, if I'm being real. Even as a tiny kid, I remember giving away my mom’s brand new clothes to a woman who came to our door asking for help. I just couldn't stand seeing someone go without. I grew up mostly in my own head, buried in Bengali stories and mystery novels. My parents were teachers, so they always pushed me to paint, write, and play sports, which I think is the only reason I eventually opened up. 

But as I got older, I started noticing how different the world was for women, and it just didn't sit right with me. It felt unfair, you know? I joined the Scouts and university debate just to see different points of view. But Dhaka... that was the real shock. Seeing people living on the street in total, crushing poverty really hit me. I’d just sit and talk to them, and one day I found out they couldn't even afford a simple white cloth for a burial. They couldn't even get their loved ones back to their villages. It was heartbreaking. Truly. I knew right then I couldn't just sit there. That’s what pushed me into the Students Union and later the NPA. I’m still on that path today, and honestly, I’m not stopping.

10. describe a contribution that meaningfully improve a group or community. 

Garment workers in Bangladesh are basically the backbone of this whole country, but they get treated like they don't matter at all. They work insane hours and half the time don't even get their pay on time. In 2023, when I was treasurer for the Students Union, I was seeing this struggle every single day. It wasn't just a news headline for me—it was real lives, real families. 

So I jumped into the minimum wage movement. I was on the ground with them, mostly just listening but also helping them write placards and talking to reporters so their side actually got out there for once. I also spent a lot of time trying to get other folks in civil society to step up and show some support. We were pushing for 23,000 Taka. We didn't get all of it, but we pressured the government enough to move the wage from 8,000 to 12,500. It wasn't perfect, not even close, but it gave a lot of people a little room to breathe. And honestly? They deserve so much more dignity than that.

11. reflect on a challenge that changed you, how did you grow from it? 

Man, the backlash. That was the real test. About two years into my activism, I jumped into this anti-rape movement, and suddenly my face was everywhere in the news. I didn’t see the personal side of it coming, you know? It got so heavy, so fast. My own family—people I’d known literally since I was a baby—started telling me I was "too much" or that I shouldn't be doing this. One relative even banned me from her house. Just like that. Banned.

Then the cyberbullying started. And it was non-stop. Every time I’d post a photo or do an interview, it was just this wave of hate. At first, it totally wrecked me. I was having these massive highs when a protest went well, and then I'd go home and just feel completely crushed by the online noise. But eventually, I realized—I can’t just stop. This is what I’m meant to do. I started reading about my role models and saw they went through the exact same mess. That gave me the boost I needed. I leaned on my friends and just worked harder. Now? Those relatives have mostly come around, and the social media noise? I barely even notice it anymore. It taught me how to stay steady in a crisis.

12. how do you typically handle conflict in a group?

Honestly, fixing group drama is really just about knowing how to actually talk to people without making them feel defensive... even when they're angry. It's different every time, but as an organizer, I have this little routine I follow. When a conflict blows up, I pull together a tiny group of five people—including me—to keep things neutral and balanced. 

First, I meet both sides separately. And I just listen. I don't judge, I just let them talk as long as they need to. I try to calm everyone down and get the actual facts on paper so we aren’t just arguing about feelings. Then the committee looks at everything to find a middle ground or a real solution. I act as the mediator when we finally bring everyone together. If it’s just a misunderstanding, we clear it up. If someone was actually wrong, I help them own it and apologize. But if it’s a big rule break or some serious legal issue, I have to be the one to lay out the consequences clearly. Keeping it collaborative is the only way I've been able to keep teams from falling apart when things get intense.

13. describe a time you took initiative without being asked what did you do? 

July 2024. The Quota movement—which, honestly, turned into a full-blown uprising. I was out there prototyping... wait, no, I mean protesting... with the public university students in Dhaka. They were super organized at places like Shahbagh. But I noticed the private university students were just... totally disconnected and scattered. It hit me like a bolt of lightning that if we didn't pull them in, it was 2018 all over again and we’d fail.

So I just started networking. Non-stop. From July 6th to the 9th, I was basically living on my phone, reaching out to everyone I could find. By the 10th, I’d pull together the first unified group in Rampura with only 40 students. It exploded. By the 16th? We had over 2,000 students at Rampura Bridge. Then July 18th happened, and the police started shooting. We lost over 40 private university students that day. Seeing that... it was horrifying. I was getting death threats, but I stayed out there, coordinating with over 30 universities until the government finally fell on August 5th. I ended up being a main coordinator for the whole thing. It was a brutal lesson, but it showed me I can lead even when the world is literally burning around me.

14. describe a time you work with people different from you. 

Back in 2017, when I was just starting uni, a friend mentioned the Jaago Foundation. I really wanted to help out, so I signed up to volunteer. For about six months, I was a teacher at their school in the Korail slum in Gulshan. It was a completely different world from anything I’d ever known. 

Those kids didn't have much at all—no big houses, no high-tech toys—but that school was their only real shot at a better life. I’d go there twice a week and just get to know them and their world. I taught the younger kids, and we’d make crafts or go play in this little field nearby. Honestly? Those kids were so happy and full of life that it actually made me feel peaceful just being around them. What really stayed with me was seeing them get a real chance to move up and out of that situation. Knowing I helped, even a little bit, felt amazing. And just recently, I heard that one of my old students actually got a full scholarship to go abroad. I can’t even tell you how proud that makes me. It makes all that time in the school feel 100% worth it.

15. tell us something new you learn recently that change your perspective. 

Okay, so Oslo. Last February. I was there for a seminar at the university, right? But the thing that actually shifted my brain wasn't anything an academic said. It was this old guy at a market. He was selling books out of this rusty old trailer. I’m standing there, and I can see he’s paralyzed on his left side. He’s struggling with these heavy baskets, and I just... instinctively... go to help him. 

Big mistake. He starts screaming 'Go back! Go back!' in my face. I was mortified. Everyone was staring. But I just... stayed quiet. I paid for my book, asked for some wrap, and just finished up. Then, suddenly, his whole mood flips. He asks if I’m an activist because I didn’t yell back at him. He actually apologized and told me how proud he was of being independent even with the paralysis. He even gave me a book of poems. It taught me that patience isn't just a "nice thing"—it’s a way to let people keep their dignity. It showed me a positive vibe can fix a total disaster, no matter how bad it starts.

16. what leadership skill do you want to improve the most, why? 

Actually, the thing I’m most hungry to fix is my ability to build a team that doesn't just... shatter. I've seen it happen. You have a great goal, but if the chemistry is off, the project is dead on arrival. I want to move from just "being the leader" to being the person who builds the foundation. Finding that harmony between people who all have their own baggage? That is way harder than any policy paper. 

And I want to be better at help people find their "internal engine." So many folks wait for an external "why," and as soon as that's gone, they quit. If I can help them find their own reason to care, the team stays solid even when things get rocky. Plus, I need to master flexibility. When things go sideways—and they will!—it’s too easy to just freeze up. Being able to pivot without losing your momentum is huge. This isn't just about one project; it’s about building something that outlasts me. A team that’s happy and creative? That’s the dream.

17. what is the most urgent problem in your community and why does it matter to you. 

Look, if you want me to be 100% real: our biggest problem is that people don't know how to be "citizens." We have zero civic literacy. None. When you don't know your rights or how the system is supposed to work, everything just... rots. I see it with the trash. People dump it anywhere because they don't feel like the community belongs to them. Then the rains come, mosquitoes breed, and suddenly everyone’s sick with dengue. Including me, once. 

It’s also about how we treat each other. I remember coming home late from the hospital and the traffic was just... a nightmare. The gate was locked, and a neighbor just started screaming at me for being out late. Not "are you okay?" or "need a hand?" Just yelling. That lack of basic courtesy? It was a shock. Plus, people miss out on help because they don't even know it exists—like the government centers that sell seeds for pennies. Most folks don't even know they have the right to ask for transparency. Fixing this "citizen gap" is the only thing that’s going to make our community actually work.

18. what challenges facing the wider Muslim world motivate you to act and what role do you want to play? 

The Muslim world is in such a complicated spot right now. Three big things keep me up at night. First, we’re just... drifting. It feels like we’re becoming more fragmented every day, stuck in our own little bubbles instead of being a unified community. We need actual ways to talk across those lines. 

Second, we’ve lost our intellectual edge. We used to lead in math and science, but now we’re trailing. It makes it hard to have any real impact on the global stage. And finally, the treatment of Muslim minorities? It’s heartbreaking. I want to be part of the generation that fixes that. I want to help bring back that culture of learning and unified dialogue. We have to stop thinking as fragments and start moving as a collective again. That’s the only way forward I see.

19. what leadership quality do you believe the world needs more of right now and why does it matter to you? 

I’m convinced the world needs "biocultural leadership." I know it sounds like a mouthful, but it’s just common sense. It’s leading in a way that knows human progress and nature are the same story. Our tech, our culture—they're all part of the same ecosystem keeping us alive. We’re in this mess because we forgot that. 

We need industry, but we can’t have it melting the ice caps and trashing our future. We need leaders who can "thread the needle"—making policies that keep us prosperous without killing the planet. Nature isn't a resource to be looted; it’s a partner. If we don't start dealing with the actual causes of our problems instead of just patching the surface, we’re doomed. If people see a leader who actually cares about both the people and the planet? They’ll follow. It’s the only path that makes sense.
